52Contract Gen: Verification-Driven OCL Contract Generation
Generating contract-level Object Constraint Language (OCL) specifications from natural-language requirements remains a challenging
task. Although large language models can produce plausible OCL fragments, the results often contain syntactic errors, inconsistencies
with the target model, or unclear semantic correctness under validation. As a result, existing one-shot generation approaches struggle
to produce OCL operation contracts that are correct, executable, and robust under downstream checking.
This paper presents Contract Gen, a verification-driven multi-agent framework for OCL operation contract generation. Rather than
relying on one-shot prompting or a single monolithic generator, Contract Gen coordinates four specialized agents:OCL Generator,
Contract Generator,TypeScript Generator, andTypeScript Parser. These agents cooperate with deterministic validation stages
including OCL parsing, OCL-to-TypeScript translation, TypeScript checking, and Jest-based execution. When parsing or TypeScript
validation fails, the returned diagnostics are fed back to the OCL Generator to guide another revision round within a bounded budget.
To move beyond surface-level text plausibility, we further support execution-grounded validation through OCLVM, a lightweight
backend that translates generated contracts into executable TypeScript and provides source-aware diagnostic evidence, including
localized runtime feedback for failing sub-expressions. Parser and TypeScript diagnostics are used for automatic feedback-guided
revision, while Jest execution results serve as final validation evidence. We evaluate the proposed approach on representative case
studies. The results show that, compared with non-iterative generation settings, Contract Gen improves both the validity and
the execution-grounded correctness of generated OCL contracts. Overall, this work offers a practical path from natural-language
requirements to executable and verifiable OCL operation contracts.

1 Introduction
Model-driven development (MDD) helps manage the complexity of modern software systems by raising the level of
abstraction [ 10,16]. Within MDD, the Object Constraint Language (OCL) plays a central role in specifying precise
semantic constraints and operation contracts that cannot be captured by structural models alone [ 9,29]. In practice,
however, authoringcontract-levelOCL remains difficult. Writing preconditions, postconditions, and invariants is time-
consuming, error-prone, and highly sensitive to model context, including types, navigation paths, collections, and
undefined values [ 1,4,30]. These challenges make it difficult to derive OCL operation contracts from natural-language
requirements in a form that is not only syntactically well-formed, but also executable and verifiable.
Large language models (LLMs) provide a promising direction for automating OCL generation from natural-language
requirements [ 1,30]. However, one-shot generation is often unreliable. Generated contracts may be syntactically invalid,
inconsistent with the target metamodel, or semantically brittle under execution, which is consistent with broader
observations on the correctness limitations of LLM-based code generation [ 7,12,13]. Moreover, when a generated
contract fails, existing workflows usually provide limited support for systematically incorporating validation evidence
into subsequent generation rounds. As a result, subsequent attempts often degenerate into unconstrained repeated
regeneration rather than feedback-guided revision [ 11,19,27]. This gap suggests that OCL contract generation should
be treated not merely as a text generation problem, but as a verification-driven synthesis task.
To address this challenge, we presentContract Gen, a verification-driven multi-agent framework for generating
executable OCL operation contracts from natural-language requirements and model context. Rather than relying
on one-shot prompting or a single monolithic generator, Contract Gen organizes contract synthesis as an iterative

2 Related Work
This section situates our work with respect to three closely related lines of research:
(1) generating OCL specifications from natural-language requirements
(2) improving generation reliability through iterative or agentic refinement
(3) execution backends for validating OCL specifications at runtime.
2.1 Natural-Language-to-OCL Generation
Prior work on generating OCL from natural language has progressed from rule-based pipelines to learning-based
and, more recently, LLM-based methods [ 1,2,4,30]. A central challenge across this line of research is to produce
specifications that are not only syntactically valid, but also consistent with the target model context and usable at the
granularity required in practice, namelyoperation contractsrather than isolated statements or expressions. Table 1
summarizes representative approaches.
Rule- and pattern-based approaches. Early studies commonly relied on handcrafted linguistic rules, templates, or
intermediate formal representations. Bajwa and Shahzada compared NLP-based and pattern-based OCL generation
strategies and proposed an SBVR-based pipeline for mapping natural-language constraints into semi-formal representa-
tions that can later be transformed into OCL [ 4]. These approaches demonstrate feasibility, but they typically assume
relatively structured inputs and still require substantial manual proofreading in realistic scenarios [4].
Learning-based methods. Learning-based methods reduce manual engineering effort and relax restrictions on
input form. DeepOCL by Yang et al. generates OCL constraints from unstructured natural-language descriptions,
improving flexibility over rigid rule-based pipelines [ 30]. However, such methods may still suffer from context-related

LLM-based generation and prompt engineering. The emergence of large language models has substantially strength-

2.2 Iterative Refinement, Agentic Generation, and Reliability
A second line of related work concerns how to improve the reliability of LLM outputs through decomposition, iterative
reasoning, and structured feedback. This line is particularly relevant because OCL contract generation is not merely a
text generation task: it also requires consistency with metamodel context, type rules, and executable semantics.
Reasoning decomposition and agentic workflows. Chain-of-Thought prompting and self-consistency have been
shown to improve performance on complex reasoning tasks by decomposing intermediate steps and reducing brittle
single-pass decisions [ 14,25,26]. More broadly, recent agentic frameworks suggest that complex generation tasks can
benefit from role decomposition, tool use, and iterative decision making rather than one-shot prompting [ 21,24,31,32].
These ideas motivate decomposing OCL contract synthesis into specialized roles for drafting, checking, and revision.
Reliability limitations of one-shot generation. A large body of evidence shows that LLM-generated code may be fluent
yet incorrect, incomplete, or hallucinated [ 7,12,13]. This observation directly applies to OCL: a generated contract may
look syntactically plausible while remaining ill-typed, context-inconsistent, or semantically incorrect during execution.
Improving reliability therefore requires not only better prompts, but also structured validation signals that can support
targeted revision.
From detection to revision-oriented feedback. Execution-based validation is more aligned with practical usability
than surface-level textual similarity alone. However, merely detecting that a generated artifact fails is often insufficient
for iterative refinement. To support stable revision, the system should indicate where the failure occurs and, ideally,
which clause or sub-expression is responsible. This requirement is particularly important for OCL contracts, where a
local mistake in a predicate, navigation, or quantifier can invalidate an entire precondition or postcondition.
Our work builds on the broader idea that complex generation tasks benefit from decomposition and iterative
feedback, but specializes this idea to OCL contract synthesis. In particular, Contract Gen combines structured LLM-
based generation with validation signals from parsing and TypeScript-based checking, so that failed candidates can be
regenerated under explicit diagnostic guidance rather than through unconstrained repeated prompting. In addition,
OCLVM provides localized runtime diagnostics that improve the interpretability of execution outcomes and motivate
future extensions toward tighter revision loops.

2.3 OCL Translation, Compilation, and Execution Backends
Executable support for OCL has long been important in model-driven engineering, where OCL is used to express
constraints, queries, and derivation rules [ 9,10,16]. In practice, translating OCL into executable artifacts such as
bytecode or target-language code remains a common strategy for runtime checking and downstream integration
[11,19,27,29]. However, many existing solutions remain closely tied to specific technology stacks and are not designed
primarily to provide revision-oriented feedback for closed-loop generation workflows. Table 2 compares representative
OCL-to-code toolchains.
Eclipse/EMF-centric execution. The OCL virtual machine (OCLVM) by Willink et al. compiles OCL expressions into
Java bytecode for efficient JVM-based execution [ 27]. RM2PT by Yang et al. employs Xtext for OCL syntax definition
and Xtend templates for code generation, translating OCL constraints into Java service interfaces and implementations
within a requirements-driven prototype generation workflow [ 5,8,28,29]. These systems demonstrate the feasibility of
executable OCL, but they remain strongly tied to the Java/Eclipse ecosystem [20, 27–29].
Cross-platform and alternative execution settings. CrossEcore compiles OCL expressions defined over Ecore into
multiple target languages, including Java, C#, Swift, TypeScript, and JavaScript, thereby broadening deployment
possibilities [ 19]. VMTS departs from Java/Eclipse by compiling OCL constraints into .NET runtime code via ANTLR
and CodeDOM [ 11,23]. These approaches show that executable OCL support can move beyond a single platform, yet
they do not focus on providing a unified validation backend with localized diagnostic feedback for generation-revision
loops.
Common limitations and motivation. Across execution-oriented approaches, two recurring limitations remain. First,
semantic fidelity is often fragile for richer OCL constructs such as collections, navigation, and undefined handling. Second,
runtime diagnostics are usually coarse-grained, often indicating only that a contract failed rather than pinpointing the
responsible sub-expression [ 11,19,27,29]. These limitations matter even more in our setting because verification-driven
contract generation depends on a backend that is not only executable, but also informative enough to support iterative
revision. Accordingly, OCLVM is positioned in our work not as an isolated end in itself, but as a validation backend
that enables execution-grounded and localized feedback for Contract Gen.
Taken together, the literature reveals a gap at the intersection of these three directions. Natural-language-to-OCL
methods have improved generation capability, agentic workflows have improved iterative reasoning, and executable
OCL backends have enabled runtime checking; however, these strands have rarely been brought together to support
contract-level,verification-driven, andrevision-orientedOCL generation. Our work addresses this gap through Contract
Gen, a multi-agent framework grounded in executable validation and fine-grained feedback.

3 Approach
3.1 Contract Gen: Verification-Driven Multi-Agent Synthesis
The core idea of Contract Gen is to couple multi-agent LLM-based contract generation with a sequence of validation steps,
so that each revision round is grounded in diagnostic evidence rather than surface-level textual plausibility alone. This
design improves controllability over one-shot prompting and turns OCL contract generation into a verification-driven
synthesis task.
Workflow decomposition.In the current implementation, Contract Gen is organized as a compact multi-agent closed-
loop workflow with four specialized agents and several deterministic validation stages. The agents share the same
contract state but play different roles:
(1)OCL Generatorinterprets the natural-language requirement and system input, performs semantic understanding
and clause planning internally, and drafts structured OCL fields for the definition, precondition, and postcondition
(2)Contract Generatorassembles the generated fields into a complete OCL operation contract and invokes the
OCL parser to produce either a parsed OCL AST or parser diagnostics
(3)TypeScript Generatortranslates the parsed OCL contract into executable TypeScript artifacts, including the
entry file and related entity or service code
(4)TypeScript Parserperforms TypeScript-level static checking and returns diagnostics when the generated code
is syntactically or semantically invalid.
The deterministic backend then acts as the verification environment that accepts or rejects each candidate and
returns structured evidence to the agent team.
Requirement-conditioned contract synthesis.Given an operation description, parameter list, return type, and project
context, the OCL Generator organizes the relevant context for the target operation. It then decomposes the operation
semantics into contract-level obligations and generates a candidate contract in structured form, includingdefinition,
precondition, andpostconditionfields.
Contract assembly and parsing.The generated fields are assembled into a complete OCL operation contract and
checked by the OCL parser. This stage determines whether the candidate is syntactically well formed and produces
parsing diagnostics when the contract cannot be accepted.
Code translation and TypeScript-based checking.If parsing succeeds, the contract is translated into executable
TypeScript through OCLVM. The generated TypeScript is then checked through compiler-based analysis to detect
malformed expressions, incompatible types, and unresolved members before execution.
Execution-based validation.If the translated code passes TypeScript-based checking, it is executed under the cor-
responding Jest-based test scenarios. This stage provides execution-grounded evidence about whether the generated
contract behaves consistently with the expected operation semantics and serves as the final acceptance criterion in the
current workflow.
Closed-loop revision.Figure 1 shows the overall workflow. Starting from a requirement input, Contract Gen first
produces an initial contract candidate. The candidate is then successively processed by parsing, translation, and
TypeScript-based checking. When parsing or TypeScript validation fails, the resulting diagnostic information is fed
back to the OCL Generator, which generates a revised contract in the next iteration. If these loop-integrated stages
succeed, the translated code is further evaluated through execution-based validation. A contract is accepted when it
satisfies the workflow’s validation criteria within the bounded revision budget.

3.2 OCLVM: Execution-Grounded Validation Backend
OCLVM serves as the validation backend of Contract Gen. Its role is not merely to execute generated contracts, but to
provide execution-grounded evidence about whether a contract is parsable, translatable, statically checkable, executable,
and diagnostically actionable. Compared with prior executable OCL backends such as the JVM-based OCLVM by Willink
et al., RM2PT, CrossEcore, and .NET-based OCL compilation, the OCLVM backend in Contract Gen is designed as a
lightweight Web-oriented validation backend that can be directly integrated into a closed-loop generation-and-revision
workflow [11, 19, 27–29].
Backend pipeline.Conceptually, OCLVM realizes three backend phases:
(1) parsing the OCL contract into a typed abstract syntax tree (AST)
(2) translating the AST into executable TypeScript using explicit mapping rules and a supporting runtime library
(3) validating the generated code through static checking and dynamic execution.
In the current implementation, these phases appear as a five-step pipeline inside Contract Gen, spanning contract
generation, contract assembly and parsing, TypeScript generation, TypeScript static checking, and final test execution.
Current implementation view.Figure 2 details how OCLVM is currently integrated into Contract Gen. The OCL
Generator first produces the three contract parts in structured form. These parts are then assembled into a complete
OCL operation contract and parsed by the ANTLR4-based front end. If parsing succeeds, OCLVM translates the parsed
contract into an executable TypeScript entry file, which is subsequently analyzed by the TypeScript static checker and
finally executed under Jest-based tests.
Parsing and intermediate representation.We implement the front end using ANTLR4 and transform parse trees into a
typed AST via a visitor-based pipeline [ 18]. The AST explicitly represents OCL constructs relevant to contract execution,
including navigation, iterator expressions, collection operations, conditionals, and Boolean connectives defined in OCL
[9]. At this stage, parser-level diagnostics such as line/column information and error messages can be collected and
returned as structured feedback.
Semantics-aware translation.From the AST, OCLVM generates TypeScript according to a dedicated OCL-to-TypeScript
mapping strategy. The translation is designed to preserve key OCL semantics needed for contract validation, including
(1) type-related behavior for primitive types, user-defined types, and undefined values
(2) collection semantics, including collection-valued navigation and iterator operators
(3) control semantics, especially short-circuit evaluation for Boolean connectives and branch behavior for conditional
expressions.
The target language is TypeScript, which provides a practical typed checking layer before execution [6, 22].
To support these mappings, OCLVM provides a lightweight runtime library for OCL-standard operations that do not
directly correspond to native JavaScript/TypeScript constructs, such as definedness checks, type predicates, selected
collection helpers, and contract-oriented utility functions. Temporal expressions are implemented through Day.js-based

Validation modes.OCLVM combines static and dynamic validation. Static validation uses TypeScript parsing, compi-
lation, and type checking to detect malformed expressions, missing members, and incompatible types before execution
[6,22]. Dynamic validation executes generated contracts under test scenarios and records concrete validation outcomes
through Jest-based tests [ 17]. Together, these two levels provide evidence not only that a contract fails, but also whether
the failure originates from parsing, translation, static checking, or runtime behavior.
Current feedback boundary.In the current implementation, parser diagnostics, TypeScript generation errors, and
TypeScript static diagnostics are integrated into the revision loop as structured revision signals. By contrast, Jest
outcomes currently serve as the final acceptance criterion rather than as automatically loop-integrated feedback. Parser
and TypeScript diagnostics currently drive automatic feedback-guided revision, whereas Jest outcomes and localized
runtime diagnostics are used to judge and interpret the final candidate. This boundary is explicitly shown in Figure 2.

Step 2: contract assembly and parsing.The three generated fields are assembled into a complete OCL operation

executable TypeScript according to the OCL-to-TypeScript mapping rules described in Section 3. The generated entry file
is then analyzed by the TypeScript static checker. This step detects translation-level and typing-related inconsistencies
before runtime execution, such as unresolved members, incompatible expressions, or malformed generated code.
Step 4: execution-grounded validation.If the generated TypeScript passes static checking, the contract is executed
under the corresponding Jest-based tests. At this stage, the system obtains execution-grounded evidence about whether
the candidate contract behaves consistently with the intended operation semantics. A successful candidate therefore
satisfies not only syntactic and static criteria, but also the final executable validation criterion.
Step 5: diagnostic interpretation.When a candidate fails before execution, the system obtains parser- or TypeScript-
level diagnostics that are reused by the OCL Generator in the next generation round. When a candidate reaches execution
but still fails semantically, OCLVM can further provide localized runtime evidence at the sub-expression level. In the
ATM inputCard case, this includes short-circuit-aware reporting of the failing precondition component, which improves
interpretability and illustrates how execution-grounded evidence can support future finer-grained revision.

4 Evaluation
4.1 Research Questions
We evaluate the multi-agentContract Genworkflow from three complementary perspectives:
(1) whether it can generatevalidOCL contracts
(2) whether these contracts remainsuccessful under execution-grounded validation
(3) whether structured feedback and iterative revision materially improve generation quality.
Because our goal is not merely surface-form similarity butexecutable and verifiablecontract synthesis, in line with
executable evaluation practices in code generation [7, 13], we study the following research questions:
RQ1 (Syntactic Validity).How effective is Contract Gen at generating syntactically valid OCL contracts before
execution-grounded validation?
RQ2 (Execution-Grounded Validation).How effective is Contract Gen at generating OCL contracts that remain
valid after translation, compilation, and execution-based validation?
RQ3 (Feedback Utility).To what extent do multi-agent feedback and OCLVM-guided loop correction improve
generation quality and repair intermediate errors?

budget. Execution tests are still run as the final evaluation criterion, but their outcomes are not used to drive an
additional automatic revision step in the current implementation.

5 Discussion and Threats to Validity
Implications for executable OCL contract synthesis.The evaluation suggests that executable OCL contract generation
should be treated as a verification-driven engineering task rather than a pure text-generation problem. Across the
benchmark, Contract Gen improves both validity and execution-grounded correctness by combining multi-agent
synthesis with a lightweight validation backend. In particular, the no-feedback ablation indicates that structured signals
from parsing, typing, execution, and localized failure diagnosis are not merely auxiliary debugging information, but
an essential mechanism for making iterative revision effective. This result supports the broader claim of the paper:
generating contract-level OCL reliably requires a closed loop ofgenerate–validate–localize–revise, rather than one-shot
prompting alone, which complements recent prompt-based and LLM-assisted OCL generation studies [1, 2].
Dataset scale and external validity.Although the benchmark spans five representative RM2PT case studies and
multiple domains, it contains only 114 operations in total [ 28,29]. This scale does not fully capture the diversity of
industrial requirements, modeling styles, naming conventions, and ambiguity patterns. Moreover, our benchmark is
drawn from the RM2PT family rather than being fully identical to those used in all prior OCL-generation studies,
which limits strict head-to-head comparability. Future work should therefore evaluate the approach on larger and more
heterogeneous datasets, ideally including industrial artifacts and publicly reusable benchmarks.
Oracle quality and evaluation depth.Our correctness metric is grounded in translation, compilation, and Jest-based
execution tests, which is stronger than textual similarity alone and more consistent with executable evaluation practices
in code generation [ 7,13]. However, it is still bounded by the quality of the available test oracles. The current evaluation
is mainly oriented toward functional scenarios and happy-path behavior, and therefore may underrepresent boundary
conditions, invalid states, exceptional flows, and concurrency-related behaviors. Although statement and line coverage
are high, subtle semantic defects may still remain hidden in under-tested branches. Future work should strengthen the
oracle through negative testing, fuzzing, metamorphic relations, differential testing, and broader scenario coverage.
Toolchain and model dependence.The reported results are obtained under a specific toolchain configuration, namely
OCLVM together with TypeScript and Jest, and performance also depends on the capabilities of the underlying LLM
family. Different runtimes, alternative OCL engines, or different model ecosystems may affect absolute performance.
Similarly, prompt sensitivity and domain-vocabulary mismatch may vary across LLM families, especially when moving

real adoption and should be incorporated in future work together with budget-aware scheduling, caching, and more
automated model-to-code alignment.
6 Conclusion
This paper addresses a practical challenge in model-driven engineering: generatingcontract-levelOCL specifications
from requirements in a way that is not only syntactically well formed, but also executable, verifiable, and amenable
to iterative improvement. To this end, we presentedContract Gen, a verification-driven multi-agent framework for
executable OCL contract generation. Rather than treating OCL generation as a one-shot text-generation task, Contract
Gen organizes synthesis as a closed-loop collaboration among four agents:OCL Generator,Contract Generator,
TypeScript Generator, andTypeScript Parser, combined with parsing, translation, static checking, execution-based
validation, and feedback-guided revision.
To support this workflow, we further developedOCLVM, a lightweight TypeScript-based validation backend that
translates OCL contracts into executable TypeScript while preserving key OCL semantics, including type discipline,
collection behavior, and short-circuit evaluation. By combining ANTLR-based parsing, semantics-aware translation,
compiler-level checking, and Jest-based execution, OCLVM provides execution-grounded evidence about whether a
generated contract is not only plausible in form, but also runnable under validation.
Our evaluation on representative case studies shows that the proposed workflow improves the reliability of OCL
contract generation over prompting-based baselines, particularly in terms of syntactic validity and execution-grounded
validation success under iterative refinement. The no-feedback ablation further highlights the importance of structured
validation signals in guiding subsequent regeneration. Taken together, these results suggest that practical OCL contract
generation should be treated as a verification-driven engineering process rather than a surface-form generation problem
alone.
Overall, this work provides a practical path from natural-language requirements to executable and verifiable OCL
contracts. By coupling contract generation with a lightweight executable validation backend, Contract Gen makes
